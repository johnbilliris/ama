# Multilingual content authoring

Australia's emergency information must reach communities in the
languages they actually use. The AMA Hub treats multilingual content
as a first-class concern: every operator-facing string can be
authored in multiple languages, the Help corpus declares language
attributes that screen readers and translation tools rely on, and the
public-alert pipeline composes per-language alerts under a single
correlation ID. This article is the **content-author and operator
reference** for multilingual authoring across all three surfaces.

If you are an operator who only **consumes** translated content
(e.g., a Public Information Officer reviewing a translated alert),
read §6 (Reviewing translated alerts) and §9 (Common pitfalls)
first.

## 1. Languages the Hub supports

The Hub's authoring and rendering pipeline supports the following
languages, in the order they are typically prioritised in Australian
emergency communications:

1. **English** (`en-AU`) — primary; every artefact must have an
   English version.
2. **Mandarin** (`zh-Hans-AU`, simplified) — major metropolitan
   communities.
3. **Arabic** (`ar`) — RTL; major metropolitan communities.
4. **Vietnamese** (`vi`) — major metropolitan communities.
5. **Greek** (`el`) — established communities, especially Melbourne
   and Sydney.
6. **Italian** (`it`) — established communities, especially
   Melbourne.
7. **Hindi** (`hi`) — growing communities, primarily Sydney and
   Melbourne.
8. **Tagalog** (`tl`) — growing communities.
9. **Punjabi** (`pa`) — growing communities, primarily Melbourne.
10. **Spanish** (`es`) — growing and established.
11. **First Nations languages** — represented as a category; per-
    language sub-codes follow ISO-639-3 (e.g., `aus-y137` for
    Yolŋu Matha). Each language is on-boarded individually with
    community consent and a nominated cultural reviewer.

The catalogue is configurable per tenant; the production-deployed
catalogue is published at `/api/help/i18n/catalogue` and visible on
the Governance Dashboard's *Localisation* panel.

## 2. The `lang` attribute everywhere

Every renderable surface in the Hub stamps a `lang` attribute on
the wrapping HTML element (`<html lang="en-AU">`) and on every
translated subtree (`<section lang="zh-Hans-AU">`, etc.). This is
not cosmetic:

- Screen readers (NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver) switch voice profiles on
  `lang` changes; without correct attributes, they read non-English
  content with English phonotactics, which is unintelligible.
- Translation tools (browser, organisational MT) detect source
  languages from `lang` rather than guessing; mis-tagged content
  produces wrong translations.
- Spell-checkers respect `lang` per-region; an `en-AU`-tagged
  article is not flagged for *organisation* vs *organization*.

The Help authoring linter (`tools/help/lint_lang_attributes.py`)
enforces:

- Every `<lang>` change in MDX must be in a wrapping element with a
  valid `lang` attribute.
- The wrapping element's tag must be a structural element
  (`section`, `aside`, `figure`, `blockquote`, `p`) — not a
  presentational one (`span`, `b`, `i`).
- The `lang` value must be in the supported catalogue.

Violations are CI errors, not warnings.

## 3. Authoring in MDX

Help articles are MDX. To author a multi-language article, organise
content as parallel sections per language, each wrapped in a
language-tagged structural element:

```mdx
<section lang="en-AU">

## English summary

This is the English text.

</section>

<section lang="zh-Hans-AU">

## 中文摘要

这是中文文本。

</section>

<section lang="ar" dir="rtl">

## ملخص بالعربية

هذا هو النص العربي.

</section>
```

Note the `dir="rtl"` attribute on the Arabic section — RTL
languages require both `lang` and `dir`. The renderer flips the
text direction within the wrapping element only; surrounding
chrome remains LTR.

The Help build pipeline emits one rendered HTML page per article
(not per language); language switching is client-side via the
language picker in the page header.

## 4. Frontmatter

Multilingual articles declare their available languages in
frontmatter:

```yaml
---
slug: my-article
title: "My article"
status: Active
classification: OFFICIAL
languages: [en-AU, zh-Hans-AU, ar, vi]
primary_language: en-AU
review_required:
  zh-Hans-AU: cultural-reviewer-zh
  ar: cultural-reviewer-ar
---
```

The `languages` array drives the language picker. The
`primary_language` is what the assistant grounds against for
non-grounding-cited prose; non-primary languages are surfaced via
parallel citations when available. The `review_required` mapping
binds each non-primary language to a nominated reviewer; without
the reviewer's sign-off, the snapshot publish gate refuses the
article.

## 5. Authoring public alerts

Public alerts go through the **Alert Composer**. Multilingual
alerts are composed via the Composer's language tab strip:

1. Author the English alert first. The Composer enforces this
   ordering — the English version is the doctrinal source.
2. Click the *+* tab and choose a target language. The Composer
   pre-fills with a draft from the organisational MT service
   (Azure Translator, AU region), labelled *"Machine draft —
   review before approval"*.
3. Edit the draft in the target language. The Composer surfaces
   the English source side-by-side and highlights paragraphs that
   have drifted significantly between the two (Levenshtein on
   embedded phrase-aligned tokens; threshold tunable per tenant).
4. For languages with `review_required` reviewers, the alert
   cannot be approved until the cultural reviewer signs off.
5. On publication, all approved language variants publish
   simultaneously to the appropriate channels (community radio,
   SMS gateways, social, signage). A single correlation ID binds
   all variants.

The Composer never publishes a machine-translated draft without a
human review. The doctrinal floor is human-in-the-loop for every
non-English public communication.

## 6. Reviewing translated alerts

If you are reviewing a translated alert, the workflow is:

1. Open the alert in the Composer's review pane (your workspace
   banner notifies you).
2. Read the English source first. Form your understanding of
   intent.
3. Switch to the translated language tab. Read end-to-end.
4. Use the side-by-side highlight view to verify that the
   translation preserves both the **information content** and the
   **register** (urgency, formality, instruction-vs-advisory).
5. Either *Approve* (signs off) or *Reject with rationale* (the
   author re-drafts; your rationale is audit-logged).

Cultural reviewers are not language proof-readers — they are
domain experts in the linguistic community. They are reviewing for
register, framing, and culturally-appropriate phrasing, not just
for grammatical correctness.

## 7. First Nations languages

First Nations languages are on-boarded individually with formal
community consent. The on-boarding workflow is documented in the
NEMA *First Nations Emergency Communications Protocol* (current
edition). Operationally:

- Each on-boarded language has a nominated **cultural authority**
  who is the sole approver of public communications in that
  language.
- The cultural authority's review is **strictly serial** — it
  cannot run in parallel with English approval. The English
  alert publishes first; the First Nations variant publishes when
  the cultural authority signs off.
- For ceremonial and culturally sensitive contexts (funeral
  protocols, story restrictions), the Hub permits the cultural
  authority to **decline** publication of the variant entirely;
  the English alert proceeds alone with an annotation in the
  audit trail.

## 8. Translations of help corpus content

The Help corpus is authored in English and selectively translated.
Articles tagged `safety_critical` (e.g., evacuation procedures,
public-alert authoring) **must** have translations in the top six
catalogue languages before snapshot publication. Other articles
translate opportunistically; the language picker shows only
languages actually authored.

When the assistant grounds in a non-English article, it cites the
non-English version explicitly so reviewers can verify register.

## 9. Common pitfalls

### 9.1 Mis-tagged language regions

Tagging Mandarin as `zh` (no region) instead of `zh-Hans-AU`
causes screen readers to fall back to mainland-China voice models
and renders some Australian community simplifications as oddly
formal. Always include the region.

### 9.2 RTL embedding inside LTR

Embedding an RTL phrase inline within an LTR sentence (e.g., quoting
an Arabic place name in an English paragraph) requires a
`<bdi>` wrapping element, not a `<span lang="ar">`. The `<bdi>`
element instructs the bidi algorithm to isolate the embedding; a
plain `<span>` lets it leak punctuation in surprising ways.

### 9.3 Translating measurements

Measurements should be re-formatted, not just translated. *"50 km"*
in English may become *"50 公里"* in Mandarin (no space) or
*"٥٠ كم"* in Arabic with eastern-Arabic numerals. The Composer's
*Format measurements* button does this automatically; resist the
temptation to "fix" the spacing afterwards.

### 9.4 Ambiguous English source

Ambiguity in the English source amplifies in translation. If the
English alert says "Move to higher ground" without specifying *how
high*, a translator will guess; the guesses will diverge across
languages. Author English with explicit, measurable instructions
when safety-critical.

### 9.5 Cultural reviewer turnaround

Cultural reviewers are typically community volunteers; their
turnaround is usually hours, not minutes. Plan multilingual alerts
into the operational timeline; do not assume same-minute
turnaround. For emergency-imminent alerts, the English version
publishes first and the translations follow as fast as review
allows.
